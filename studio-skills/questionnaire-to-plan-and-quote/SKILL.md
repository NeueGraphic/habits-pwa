# Questionnaire to Plan & Quote

Reads a completed client questionnaire and produces one polished, client-ready proposal.

The plan of action and the service quote are not separate documents. The plan lives in sections 2, 3 and 6. The quote lives in section 7. Everything lands in a single eight-section proposal.

Always produce the full document. Never refuse due to missing information — make stated assumptions and deliver a usable draft.

---

## The Rule

**Every proposal is eight sections. Nothing else earns a spot.**

1. The goal of the project
2. The format
3. What's included
4. How the support works
5. What it looks like at the outcome
6. The timeframe
7. The investment
8. What to do next

The order is fixed. Value is established before price. No cover letter, no studio background, no appendix.

---

## Step 1: Parse the Questionnaire

Before writing anything, read all answers in full. Identify:

- **Core objective** — What is the client actually trying to achieve? Distil to one sentence. This becomes section 1.
- **Scope signals** — What services, deliverables, or phases are implied or stated? These become sections 2 and 3.
- **Timeline indicators** — Any deadlines, urgency cues, or launch targets mentioned? These become section 6.
- **Budget indicators** — Any direct figures, ranges, or indirect signals (e.g., "small business", "fast turnaround", "premium quality")? These calibrate section 7.
- **Complexity signals** — Number of stakeholders, decision layers, technical requirements, custom requests?
- **Gaps and contradictions** — Anything vague, missing, or internally inconsistent?

Collect your findings before producing any output.

---

## Step 2: Handle Gaps and Edge Cases

**If the questionnaire is incomplete:**
- Proceed with the best available interpretation
- State assumptions inline, in the section they affect, italicised: *Assumption: ...*
- Do not create an "Assumptions & Notes" section — that would be a ninth section. Assumptions live where they apply.

**If the goal seems unrealistic given budget or timeline:**
- Say so in section 6 or 7, whichever it belongs to, in one diplomatic line
- Offer a scoped-down alternative or phased approach
- Never refuse to proceed — always deliver a workable path forward

**If answers are contradictory:**
- Note the contradiction briefly in the relevant section
- State which interpretation you've used and why

**If the gaps are severe enough to block a real number:** flag them to the user in a short note *outside* the proposal, before the document. Never inside it.

---

## Step 3: Write the Proposal

### 1. The goal of the project

One short paragraph. The client's objective in their own terms — the business outcome, not the design activity. Lead with it; no greeting, no preamble.

### 2. The format

The shape of the engagement: phases and their sequence, how work is presented, where it happens, who's doing it. Two to four sentences, or a short phase list.

This is where the plan of action's *sequence* lives. Keep it readable — the client should be able to picture the run of the project in one pass.

### 3. What's included

The deliverables, listed plainly, grouped under phase headings where the project has distinct phases. Every line is something the client receives.

Where responsibility matters, state it on the line itself (e.g. "Content supplied by client"). Do not build a separate responsibility column or table — it belongs in the line or in section 4.

Close with a short **Not included** list, three to five lines: third-party costs, out-of-scope work, anything the questionnaire hinted at but the price doesn't cover.

### 4. How the support works

How you and the client work together through the project:

- Feedback consolidated per round; state the number of revision rounds included
- What triggers a variation to the quoted price
- Who the client deals with and how quickly they hear back
- What the studio needs from them, and by when
- IP transfer on final payment

### 5. What it looks like at the outcome

One paragraph. The end state, concretely — what the client has in hand on the last day and what they can now do that they couldn't before. Specific and physical. Not a re-list of section 3.

### 6. The timeframe

Total duration, then a one-line-per-phase breakdown. Realistic, not optimistic. Close with a single line noting what the timeline depends on (feedback windows, content supply, deposit receipt).

### 7. The investment

Itemised, so the client can see what they're buying:

```
| Phase | Scope | Investment |
|---|---|---|
| [Phase 1 name] | [Brief scope note] | $X,XXX |
| [Phase 2 name] | [Brief scope note] | $X,XXX |
| **Total** | | **$XX,XXX + GST** |
```

Timeframes are not repeated in this table — they live in section 6.

**Pricing rules:**
- If enough detail exists: give a fixed price per line item
- If scope is unclear: give a clearly labelled range (e.g. `$2,000 – $3,500`) with a one-line note on what determines the final figure
- Never leave pricing blank — always give a figure or a range
- Follow the table with payment terms in two lines (deposit / balance). Do not justify the price

### 8. What to do next

A numbered list, three or four steps, each an action with a clear owner. End on the action.

---

## Tone Guidelines

- Professional, confident, and client-friendly
- Write as a trusted expert — not hedging, not overselling
- Plain English, no jargon unless the client is clearly a technical audience
- Every line serves the client's understanding
- Concise: no padding, no filler phrases
- Nothing in the document that isn't one of the eight sections

---

## Quick Reference: Common Scope Signals

Use these to calibrate complexity and pricing if the questionnaire is light on detail:

| Signal | Implication |
|---|---|
| "Small business / startup" | Lean scope, value-focused, may need education on process |
| "Established company / enterprise" | More stakeholders, longer approval cycles, higher budget tolerance |
| "ASAP / urgent" | Rush premium may apply; flag if timeline is unrealistic |
| "We have brand guidelines already" | Reduced discovery; adaptation work only |
| "We need everything from scratch" | Full-scope engagement; budget accordingly |
| "We have a small budget" | Scope down to essentials; offer phased approach |
| "Premium / high-end / luxury" | Client expects polish — do not underquote |
| Multiple locations / markets | Localisation and scale complexity |
| Regulatory or compliance mention | Additional review rounds, legal sign-off, risk buffer |

---

## Quality Check Before Outputting

1. Are there exactly eight sections, in order, with nothing extra?
2. Does section 1 state the client's goal, not the studio's process?
3. Are assumptions placed inside the sections they affect rather than collected up front?
4. Does section 5 paint the outcome rather than re-list the deliverables?
5. Does every line item in section 7 carry a figure or a labelled range?
